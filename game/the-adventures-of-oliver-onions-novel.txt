THE ADVENTURES OF OLIVER ONIONS
A Mystery in Silvora Reefs
by the Game Itself

PROLOGUE: THE SENTENCE THAT WOULD NOT END

There is a particular kind of silence that obtains in a room where a sentence has been left unfinished for eleven years. It is not the silence of absence. It is the silence of tension — the high, thin hum of a string pulled past its tuning and held there by a hand that has forgotten how to let go. The dust in such a room does not settle. It arranges itself. It forms drifts in the shape of the argument that has been rehearsed two hundred times and never delivered, each draft folded the same way, each one placed in the waste basket with the care a man gives to something he cannot bring himself to burn.

The typewriter knows. It has always known. Its keys wear down in the pattern of the hands that use them — all even, all honest, except one. The O. The O is worn shiny, down past the lettering to the bare metal, because some letters carry more weight than others, and the letter that begins your name has been struck more times than any other character in the alphabet. The ribbon has gone grey at the edges. The platen bears the ghost of a page that has not advanced in a fortnight, because the sentence on that page stops at *and he does not* and there is no verb after the negation, only the vast white space of everything that might follow.

Furthermore — the elephant, the witness, the thing older than language — shifts his weight in the corner. The air thickens. Something vast assembles behind his soft eyes, something that has been waiting since before there were words to wait in. He has been in this room since I came through the seam. He was in the harbour. He was in the kitchen with my mother. He does not speak. He has never spoken. He regards language as a fad he is not going to take up at the last minute for the convenience of two men in a small room.

The page in the machine says *and he does not*. It has said that since the moment I came through. It will go on saying it for exactly as long as there are two people in this room being people at each other.

One of us has to finish it.

I have come a long way to reach this sentence. I have walked the harbour road and the hanging corridor and the folded hallway of the Möbius. I have stood in the vault at Rest Assured and read my own name on a brass plate, including a middle name I have not spoken aloud in thirty years. I have eaten liver and onions at Chef D.'s and watched the onions arrange themselves into four rows — ten, nine, seven, and a long one across the bottom — a keyboard made of food, the O worn shiny. I have drunk gin at the Möbius and felt the room loosen around me, the impossible corridor becoming merely a feature, the dancers' paradox resolving into simple good manners. I have stood in the queue at Dude's Dews and listened to three separate people mention the rotor sound over the harbour and change the subject within four seconds, smoothly, without any of them noticing the others do it.

I have carried the weight of two hundred drafts in a basket by my foot. I have watched the ink withdraw from ninety pages of my notebook, page by page, back toward a nib that is not there.

And now I am here. Nine feet by eleven. A desk that is a door. A lamp with a green shade. A cup with a ring under it older than the cup. A chair pushed back and left pushed back. A man at a wall that is not a window, wearing green with purple accents — my colours, inverted — who has not turned round in eleven years.

The sentence waits.

The elephant waits.

Furthermore draws himself up.

And I realize that the waiting is the sentence. The waiting *is* the thing that has not been finished, that cannot be finished because finishing it would release the tension that has been building for eleven years, that would end the silence that has been holding me together, that would make me something other than a man waiting for a sentence to end.

The sentence is the waiting. The sentence is the elephant in the corner who does not speak. The sentence is the harbour water that is cold in the way only northern water can be cold. The sentence is the rotor blades that descend from a sky that never commits to crimson. The sentence is the three dry pages in the notebook that came out of the water. The sentence is the gold monocle through which I read the smeared pages that are being withdrawn back toward a nib that is not there.

The sentence is the four words that I am about to write, and the four words that I have been writing for eleven years, and the four words that I will write after these ones, and the four words that will finish it.

The sentence is the waiting. And the waiting is not nothing. The waiting is everything. The waiting is the sentence.

Furthermore draws himself up.

PART ONE: THE WATER

The sky over Silvora Reefs does not behave. It bruises slowly from violet into a crimson it never quite commits to, a wound that will not close and will not open, suspended in the perpetual evening of a town that has forgotten what morning looks like. The harbour water is cold in the way only northern water can be cold — not merely absent of heat but actively hostile to it, a cold that has intention, that enters the bones and makes a home there. I am lying on my back in the shallows, the tide working at my coat, the silt grinding against my spine, and the first thing I notice is that my monocle is intact.

The second thing is my watch. It says 3:33. It has said 3:33 for as long as I can remember, which is not very long at all — memory being one of the things the water took, along with my name, my history, the paperwork that proves a person is a person. The word *Silvora* sits at the front of my head with no paperwork to support it, a word without a visa, a squatter in the space where my identity used to live.

The third thing is the smell. Hot metal apologising. That is the only phrase for it — the scent of something that has burned and is sorry about it, the olfactory equivalent of a man clearing his throat after a shouting match. And under that, or perhaps inside it, the sound: rotor blades. Slow ones. Heavy, unhurried, entirely confident. The chop of a machine holding a position rather than travelling anywhere, somewhere off past the breakwater, in fog far too thick for any machine to be flying in.

My pockets have been gone through by the tide and are still full of gold. This is either a miracle or a message, and in Silvora Reefs the distinction is academic.

Somewhere to my left, an elephant the colour of a bruise is waiting for me to finish a sentence I do not remember starting.

I do not see him at first. The fog is too thick, the light too wrong, the sky too busy not committing to crimson. But I feel him — the displacement of air, the particular gravity of a creature that weighs several tons and carries it with the grace of a whisper. His skin is the purple of a healing contusion, the colour of the sky at the moment it cannot decide. His eyes are soft and enormously patient. He wears an ornate saddle that has never been used. He is older than language and has always regarded it as a fad.

He does not speak. He has never spoken. He stands in the corner of every room I will ever enter, and I will learn to stop asking the rooms to explain him.

My coat pockets yield a notebook.

It came out of the harbour heavy, and it has not got appreciably lighter since. Ninety pages of blue smear — ink gone entirely to weather, whole leaves fused into a single blue block at the fore-edge, the whole record of whatever I was doing before the sky handed me back reduced to a colour. The binding held. That is worth noting on its own: cheap stitching, sea water, several hours, and the block has not come out of the case.

Three pages are dry.

Not dried, which would leave a tidemark and a cockle and a stiffness in the paper. *Dry*, as though they were never in the water — flat, supple, taking a fingernail the way dry paper does. They sit in the middle of the ruin like a paragraph the flood was instructed to go around, and there is smeared blue on the page before them and smeared blue on the page after. The handwriting is mine. The words are mine in the specific sense that I would use those words, in that order, with that comma in that place. And I have no memory whatsoever of the sentence they make.

I raise the gold monocle to my eye.

Through the gold, the smeared pages stay smeared — but the blue moves. It is not ink dispersing in water. It is ink being *withdrawn*, page by page, back toward a nib, in a slow careful retraction that has been going on for some time and is nearly finished. The three dry pages are not survivors. They are what has not been taken back yet.

And now I am here. Nine feet by eleven. A desk that is a door. A lamp with a green shade. A cup with a ring under it older than the cup. A chair pushed back and left pushed back. A man at a wall that is not a window, wearing green with purple accents — my colours, inverted — who has not turned round in eleven years.

The sentence waits.

The elephant waits.

Furthermore draws himself up.

A truck crests the hill. Its headlights cut the fog like a decision.

The driver is a man named Darek Dwayne Wayne, though I do not know this yet. He is amiable, apron-clad, a drawl thick enough that his own name comes out sounding like the sign above his head — Durry Kwain, the ice cream place. He wipes the counter in the manner of a man who has decided the counter is the part of the world he can help. He has been driving this truck at four in the morning for eleven years. He has the cone made before the bell over the door has finished.

"Sorry about your wait," he says.

There has never been a wait. Durry Kwain has nine tubs, three tables, and on a busy evening four customers. It has never once had a queue in living memory. He has been saying it for eleven years.

Later, when I press him, when I stand in his shop with the chalk board revising itself behind me — prices in three decimal places, every one a second inside the minute at which my watch stopped — he will tell me: I was not saying the alphabet backwards. I was saying my own name backwards, repeatedly, all the way up the hill. *Snoino Revilo. Snoino Revilo.* Like it was somebody I was trying to warn him about.

He was already in the truck. Keys in it. Four in the morning. And he had never once thought about that until he said it aloud.

The town's plan is simple: everyone hears the rotors. Nobody says so, because in Silvora Reefs the first person to say a thing aloud makes it the case. Letting it stay a noise is the town's entire plan.

LOGIC: *Constant volume, constant bearing, four days. A machine holding a hover for four days is a machine that is not a machine — fuel is not a matter of opinion, and neither is a gearbox. Whatever is over that harbour is not flying. It is simply *there*, making the noise of a thing that flies.*

EMPATHY: *It is not that they cannot hear it. Watch a Reefer's face while the sound is happening: there is a small adjustment around the eyes, a half-beat of accommodation, and then nothing. They hear it. They have all agreed, without ever having had the conversation, to hear it as weather.*

The truck pulls away. The cone melts in my hand, tasting of a kitchen I have not stood in since my parents were alive in it, my mother at the table with her back to the door saying *and furthermore—* in my voice, my cadence, not an impression but a reading.

The elephant walks beside me up the hill. He does not speak. He does not need to.

The harbour road is packed gravel that shifts underfoot, and I find myself walking in the exact centre of it, as though the earth has been worn down by a pattern repeated every day for a generation. At the top of the hill, the fog is thinner, and I can see the harbour mouth — not water, but a grey sheet of it, stretching to where the sky and water refuse to define themselves against each other. The rotor sound is louder here, closer, and I realize with a jolt that it is not coming from above at all. It is coming from within the water, from something large and slow moving just beneath the surface.

The elephant stops beside me. He does not speak. He does not need to.

LOGIC again: *The sound originates underwater. This changes the physics entirely. Whatever is making it is not an aircraft. It is a submarine, or a sea creature of proportions that make the elephant beside me look small.*

EMPATHY: *Do not be absurd. A sea creature would not make the regular chop of a rotor blade. Whatever it is, it has learned to mimic the sound of a thing that flies. It is not mimicking. It is communicating.*

I continue up the hill. The elephant falls into step beside me. His feet make no sound on the gravel. Behind us, the harbour laps at the shore, a sound like breathing.

Durry Kwain's ice cream shop is open. The door chimes — *Ding-dong* — and the sound is wrong here, in Silvora Reefs, where nothing should sound right. Durry is behind the counter, wiping it with a rag that is already clean. He looks up as I enter.

"Welcome," he says. "Or. Welcome. Whichever. We are open though the wait is not."

He has the drawl of the harbour, a mouthful of sound that stretches vowels into syllables I did not know words could hold. Behind him, the chalk board lists prices: *Vanilla 3.005, Chocolate 2.999, Strawberry 3.001*. The decimal points stretch on forever, three numbers after the dot, never repeating, never ending.

"I'll take," I say, and stop. What do I want? The question feels foreign in my mouth.

"Take nothing," Durry says. "Or take everything. The onions are ready. They arrive on a plate, three of them, sliced into four rows. Ten. Nine. Seven. And a long one across the bottom."

He slides a plate toward me. On it are three onions, arranged exactly as he described. They smell of nothing. Of nothing, and yet — I lean in. The smell is of a kitchen I have not stood in since my parents were alive in it, my mother at the table with her back to the door saying *and furthermore—* in my voice, my cadence, not an impression but a reading.

I eat one. It tastes of nothing and everything. Of a kitchen I have not been in for eleven years. Of a mother I have not seen since the water took me. Of a father I have not spoken to since before the sky fell.

Durry watches me eat. "The town," he says, "is a place where things are held together by things that are not holding. The rotors. The silence. The first person to say a thing makes it so. You are here because the town decided you should be here. Nobody says this aloud. That is the plan."

I finish the onion. It is good. It is bad. It is onion, and I have not tasted onion in a lifetime.

The elephant is waiting at the top of the hill. We begin the walk together. He does not speak. He does not need to.

The walk up the hill is the walk that everybody in Silvora Reefs takes, and nobody takes twice the same way. The gravel shifts under our feet. The fog rolls in and out. The rotor sound is constant, a machine holding a position in the sky that is not the sky, and I find myself thinking about the alphabet backwards, about *Snoino Revilo*, about the name that is not a name.

And I think: the walk. The walk up the hill is the sentence *and he does not* made spatial. Each step is a word. The gravel shifting underfoot is the white space after the negation. The fog rolling in and out is the verb that never comes. The rotor sound constant is the negation that repeats — *and he does not, and he does not, and he does not.* The name that is not a name — *Snoino Revilo* — is the reversal that is an author. The alphabet backwards is the sentence read in reverse, the way the sentence *and he does not* reads the same forwards and backwards if you stop at the negation. The elephant walking beside me is the witness who has always been there. The elephant not speaking is the silence that is not empty but full. The elephant not needing to speak is the sentence that does not need a verb to be true. The walk up the hill is the sentence made spatial, and I have been walking it for eleven years without knowing it.

The elephant stops beside me. He does not speak. He does not need to.

LOGIC: *The walk is the walk and it is not the walk and the elephant does not speak and I do not expect him to and yet I do expect it and this is the contradiction at the heart of the sentence that has not been finished.*

EMPATHY: *He has always been there. Since before the water. Since before the sky. He is older than language and he has never needed a word to be present with me.*

We reach the hilltop. The rotor sound is loudest here. Somewhere above us, a machine holds position. It has been doing this for eleven years. Eleven years of four in the morning, of cones made before bells, of *Sorry about your wait* said into a silence that has never been a wait.

And I think: the hilltop. The highest point in Silvora Reefs. The place where the rotor sound is loudest. The place where the machine holds position. The place where the elephant turns to me and makes a sound for the first time in eleven years. A low rumble, the vibration of something ancient. The hilltop is the white space after the negation. The hilltop is where the sentence *and he does not* meets the verb that never comes. The hilltop is where the sentence waits for *It does not end.* The machine holding position is the sentence holding position — the sentence that has been holding itself together by its own teeth for eleven years. The rotor sound is the sentence speaking itself into existence every moment of every day. The elephant's rumble is the sentence acknowledging its own completion. *He is saying I am here. I have always been here. The sentence you are going to finish — it begins with this moment. With the elephant and the harbour and the sky that never commits to crimson.*

The elephant turns to me. For the first time, he makes a sound. Not words. A low rumble, the vibration of something ancient.

EMPATHY: *He is saying I am here. I have always been here. The sentence you are going to finish — it begins with this moment. With the elephant and the harbour and the sky that never commits to crimson.*

He turns away. We continue up the hill toward the town, toward the hanging building, toward the mirror in the hallway, toward the man at the wall that is not a window.

The town of Silvora Reefs spreads below me. Nine floors suspended from above. Nobody has ever explained from what. The residents treat this as a design feature. The harbour is below us, a grey sheet of water that never quite commits to being water. The rotor sound is everywhere and somewhere and nowhere.

And I think: the town. Silvora Reefs. Nine floors suspended from above. Nobody has ever explained from what. The residents treat this as a design feature because the alternative is to admit that the town hangs from a sentence, that the town is a sentence, that the town is *and he does not* made spatial, that the town is the tension of the unfinished made architectural. The harbour below us is a grey sheet of water that never quite commits to being water because the harbour is the sentence *and he does not* made liquid — the water that took ninety pages and went round three, the water that took my name and my history and the paperwork that proves a person is a person, the water that left the gold monocle and the watch and the pockets full of gold and the elephant. The rotor sound is everywhere and somewhere and nowhere because the rotor sound is the sentence speaking itself into existence, the sentence that is everywhere in the town, the sentence that is the town. Silvora Reefs spreads below me, and I see it for what it is: a sentence made architectural, a negation made geographical, a white space made harbour, a verb that never comes made rotor sound, an elephant made elephant, a typewriter made typewriter, a sentence made town.

I find myself a room. Two-F. The window has been sorted, completely sorted. Asimov Sobeleaux is in his office on the ground floor. He has a silver-streaked beard and hands that have hauled nets and inked coastlines. His office holds charts of waterways that appear on no map and blueprints that rearrange themselves when looked at sideways.

"Come in, come in — mind the corridor, it is longer today than it was contracted to be," he says.

The corridor is longer when I walk it alone. It is shorter with two. With four it is a formality. This is not mysterious, Oliver — it is *load-bearing*. A corridor exists to be between two places, and betweenness is a service, and a service scales to demand. One man needs more corridor than four men do. One man has further to go.

The explanation makes complete, satisfying, structural sense while he is saying it. I follow every step. I would have signed my name to it. And it is already going — not forgotten: *withdrawn*, actively, the way the blue is being withdrawn from my notebook. There is nothing left in the place where it was except the memory of having understood something.

I write it down before it goes. Four minutes of speech reduces to nine words: *a corridor is a service and services scale down.* That is not a summary. A summary is something you make. Nobody made this. That is what survived, and the rest did not go into the notebook and did not stay in the room and is not anywhere now.

Asimov watches me write. He pours something from a bottle that lives behind the charts and does not offer an explanation of the bottle either.

"Mm," he says. "Yes. It goes down each time and I have never had it go down in front of a witness before."

He was a cartographer before the nets, before this. A man who draws coastlines learns one thing above all others: the coastline is not the length you measure. It is the length you measure *at the scale you chose*. Change the scale, change the coast. There is no true length of Ireland. He has lived thirty years in a building where somebody keeps changing the scale. And he has never once caught the hand doing it.

"I said 'the hand'," he tells me later, when I press. "Yes. I have said it that way for years and I have never examined why, and I would rather you did not make me start at my age. — No. That is cowardice, and you came in here with wet shoes and a question, so. Here is what I have and it is not much: whatever adjusts this building does it the way a *man* does it. Not the way weather does it. It corrects. It goes back over. Twice I have found the same repair made twice, differently, as though somebody had reconsidered. And once — once, Oliver, and I have told nobody — I found it made three times, and the third one was worse than the first, and it had been put back to the first."

"The building hangs," I say. "From what?"

Asimov smiles. It is not a kind smile. It is the smile of a man who has been asked a question he has answered a thousand times and knows the answer will not be believed. "From the sentence, Oliver. The sentence that has not been finished. The sentence that stops at *and he does not* and waits for a verb that never comes. The building hangs from the tension of the unfinished. Remove the tension, and the building falls. Finish the sentence, and the building falls. The only way to keep the building standing is to keep the sentence unfinished, and the only way to keep the sentence unfinished is to keep two people in a room being people at each other, waiting for the one who will finish it."

"And if nobody finishes it?"

"Then the building stands forever. And the town stands forever. And the elephant waits forever. And the typewriter's O key wears down to the metal and the ribbon goes grey at the edges and the platen does not advance and the dust arranges itself into drifts in the shape of the argument that has been rehearsed two hundred times and never delivered. That is the plan. That has always been the plan."

Lying Landy, the Unhandy Dandy, does not fix things. Landy files.

Pinned to the corridor board in a fan of municipal beige is the complete record of his attentions to this building: WINDOW 2F — WEATHER NONCONFORMING — RESOLVED. DOOR 2C — OPENS TO 3A ON THURSDAYS — RESOLVED. STAIR, THIRD FLIGHT — COUNT — RESOLVED. CORRIDOR — LENGTH — RESOLVED, RESOLVED, RESOLVED.

The window in 2F still has weather in it. The door in 2C still opens onto 3A on Thursdays, and the residents of both have arranged their Thursdays around it. The stair count will not hold still from one Tuesday to the next. The corridor has been RESOLVED twice this week, which is once more than it has been complained about, which means Landy is now filing against a schedule rather than against events.

Each form is stamped, bottom right, with a four-lobed seal and a line of small print: FILED WITH THE COALITION FOR CULPABILITY AND CONSTRUCTIVE CHANGE. NO FURTHER ACTION IS REQUIRED OF THE RESIDENT. NO FURTHER ACTION IS AVAILABLE TO THE RESIDENT.

The last two sentences are not the same sentence, and they have been printed together on every form in the fan.

I raise the monocle.

Under the gold, the word RESOLVED is not a word. It is the same three characters typed over and over on top of themselves — R, E, S, then back, then again — until the ribbon gave out and the paper gave up and agreed to be black in that shape. It is on every form. Twenty-two of them. Nothing was resolved and nothing was ever going to be; something was *insisted on*, at length, by a machine, by somebody who could not fix the corridor and could not stop filling in the box.

And I see: the forms are not records. They are the stitches holding the building together. Each RESOLVED is a suture, a loop of black thread pulled tight through paper that has given up and agreed to be black in that shape. The four-lobed seal is not a stamp of approval — it is a knot tied by a machine that does not know how to stop tying. The Coalition for Culpability and Constructive Change is not an organization. It is the name of the force that keeps the corridor from ending four feet short of Asimov's door, that keeps the window in 2F from opening onto a different weather every Thursday, that keeps the stair count from drifting into infinity. Landy does not fix things. Landy files. And in filing, he stitches. Each form is a stitch. Twenty-two stitches. The building hangs from twenty-two stitches, each one typed by a man in a very good suit who has been copying the same form for eleven years without knowing what he was copying.

Asimov lets them stamp. Before they began stamping, the corridor did not simply vary. It *ended*, sometimes, four feet short of his door, and there was nothing past the end — not a wall. Nothing. He put his hand into it once. They stamped it RESOLVED. It has never ended since. It varies, it stretches, it is impertinent — but it goes all the way to the door every single time.

So yes. He lets them. Whatever they are doing with those words, the words are holding something up.

"If the building is made of words," I say, "then so is the man standing in it telling me so."

Asimov does not flinch. He does not agree. He does something worse: he nods, slowly, the way a man nods at an arithmetic he checked long ago.

"Sobeleaux," he says. "You never did ask about the name. As above, so below — my grandmother's joke, and she thought it was about the fishing. Above the water, below the water. The same shape twice. Oliver. If the building is made of words, then so is the man standing in it telling you so. I have known that for eleven years. It has not once stopped me putting the bins out. I recommend the bins. They are the only argument against it that has ever worked."

The elephant stands in the corner of Asimov's office. The charts of waterways rearrange themselves. The blueprints breathe. The corridor outside measures itself against my footsteps and finds itself wanting.

I move into the flat. Two-F. The flat has arranged itself into the shape it uses when I have been away: chair angled toward the window, desk under its usual weight of paper, the smell of wet wool coming off the coat I have not yet taken off. The lamp is on. I do not recall leaving it on, but the lamp and I have an arrangement and it has never yet been in writing.

The green flame in the wall lamp is doing what it does. Somewhere below, a bucket is set down on tile and a squeegee starts an arc. Two flats along, a door opens onto weather that is not the weather outside — warmer, raining, smelling of cut grass in a building four streets from the sea in the middle of a fog — and closes again, and nobody comes out. The corridor smells of grass for a while afterwards.

And I think: the flat. It arranges itself. It knows when I have been away. It knows the shape it uses when I am gone. The chair angled toward the window — the window that has been sorted, completely sorted, the window that does not open onto different weather every Thursday. The desk under its usual weight of paper — the paper in drifts rather than stacks, the dozen abandoned openings to a dozen abandoned somethings, each one a single confident sentence followed by a very great deal of white. The smell of wet wool coming off the coat I have not yet taken off — the coat that has been to the harbour and back, the coat that has been gone through by the tide and still has pockets full of gold. The lamp is on. I do not recall leaving it on, but the lamp and I have an arrangement and it has never yet been in writing. The green flame in the wall lamp is doing what it does — burning without fuel, burning without heat, burning with a light that is not light but a sentence, a sentence that says *you are here, you are here, you are here* in a language older than words.

I move into the desk. It is a door on two trestles. Paper in drifts rather than stacks. A dozen abandoned openings to a dozen abandoned somethings, each one a single confident sentence followed by a very great deal of white — and the confidence is the part that stays with me, because a man who cannot start does not write like that. That is the handwriting of somebody who knows exactly how to begin and has stopped believing there is anywhere to go afterwards.

There is no typewriter on it. There has never been a typewriter on it, in eleven years, and I would know, because it is my desk and I have sat at it every day. There is, in the dust at the back edge where a hand does not go, a rectangle where the dust is thinner. Of a size. With four small round gaps at the corners.

Through the gold the rectangle is occupied. There is a typewriter there, black and heavy and mid-sentence, and it is not translucent or ghostly or any of the things the word would lead you to expect. It is simply present, and the room has been asked not to include it.

The dust around the absent machine is undisturbed on every side. Nothing was removed. The space is where something has not yet been placed.

HAND-EYE, reconstructing: *Four feet, in a rectangle nine and a quarter by eleven and a half, with the front pair set inboard — which is a carriage-return frame, which means a heavy desk machine, which means a weight no man carries down a flight of stairs alone. And the dust is undisturbed all round it. Nothing was dragged. Nothing was lifted. The rectangle is not where a machine was taken from. It is where a machine has not yet been put.*

By the window, where the light would have been best for most of the day, the boards carry a rectangle about the size of a bread oven in which the dust is a different age from the dust around it. Four small round depressions sit at its corners, pressed down into the grain over a period long enough that the wood has stopped intending to come back up. I can put a fingertip in one. It is deeper than a fingertip. Whatever stood here stood here for years and it was heavy and it had feet rather than a base, and it was placed with some care — the rectangle is square to the window rather than square to the room, which is what a man does when what matters is the light falling on the work rather than the furniture looking tidy. The boards around it are worn in a shallow arc on the near side. Somebody sat there. For a long time, in a chair that moved a little, doing something that required them to be close.

Through the gold the rectangle is not empty. There is a machine in it, and it is being used, and the man using it has his back to me and does not turn round, and the light through the window falls on both of us at once from two different times of day.

VISUAL CALCULUS, reconstructing the removal: *It went straight up. A dragged object leaves a lip of dust at the trailing edge and a scar in the wax, and there is neither, on any of four sides. Somebody lifted a machine that takes two men, vertically, without tilting it, and did not scuff a board doing it. And then the useful part, which is the dust inside the rectangle: it is *younger* than the dust outside. Not absent. Younger. The laughter at Cheeses Thrice stops arriving late. The cans on Woolbinkle's shelf stop holding. The poem's meter stops breaking on *nope.* Monsieur Albert stops waiting in the third mirror. The elephant in the corner opens his eyes. They are soft and enormously patient. He has always been here. He will always be here. He does not speak. He has never spoken. He regards language as a fad he is not going to take up at the last minute for the convenience of a man who has just finished a sentence that has been waiting for eleven years.

The typewriter's O key is worn down to the metal. The ribbon is grey at the edges. The platen has advanced, finally, the ghost of the page that has not advanced in a fortnight replaced by a new page, blank, waiting for the next sentence. The dust in the room does not settle. It arranges itself into new drifts, the shape of the argument that will be rehearsed two hundred times and never delivered, the next sentence, the one that begins with *It does not end* and waits for its own verb.

And I think: the sentence is finished, but the waiting is not over. The sentence *It does not end* is itself a sentence that does not end, a sentence that contains its own negation, a sentence that waits for its own verb. The elephant knows this. He has always known this. He opens his eyes and looks at me with the patience of a creature who has waited longer than language has existed, and his eyes say: *Now you write the next one. And the next. And the next. The waiting never ends. The sentence never ends. It does not end.*

EPILOGUE: THE CAN ON THE TABLE

There is a can on the table by the watch.

It is sealed, and it is labelled in a hand that is not mine, in careful capitals by somebody who wanted it legible to a stranger — *Third of March, NEARLY* — and it has held. Whatever is in it is still in it. I have picked it up four times this week and I have not opened it and I am not going to, and the not-opening is the entire point and always was.

And I think: the can. *Third of March, NEARLY.* The date that is not a date. The qualifier that is not a qualifier. The can that is not a can but a sentence waiting for its verb. I will never open it. The not-opening is the entire point. Some things are meant to remain unknown. Some sentences are meant to remain unfinished, even after the sentence that has been waiting for eleven years has been finished with four words that are not clever. *It does not end.* The sentence is finished. The town has stopped. The watch goes. The radio stops at eleven. The neighbour upstairs turns it off. The desk has four legs made as legs. The floor is cold in the ordinary way. The weather does what weather does. And the can sits on the table, sealed, labelled, waiting for a third of March that will always be NEARLY, never quite arriving, the way the sentence was never quite finished until it was, and the town never quite ended until it did, and the watch never quite went until it did, and I never quite woke up until I did.

The can is the only thing that has not ended. The can is the only thing that waits. The can is the sentence that comes after *It does not end.* The can is the verb that never comes, the white space after the negation, the silence that is not empty but full, thick as winter air, packed with the things that language cannot name.

I pick up the can. I hold it in my hand. It is cold — ordinary cold, not the hostile cold of harbour water. The label is smooth under my fingers. *Third of March, NEARLY.* I set it down. I do not open it. I will not open it. The not-opening is the entire point and always was.

At six o'clock, out of the habit of a fortnight, I say good evening to a piece of empty air. The air is empty. But the pause was the point. The pause was where the answer would have been, if there had been an answer, if the elephant had spoken, if language were not a fad but a necessity. In the ordinary room, the pause is longer than a pause has any business being, because there is no elephant, no green flame, no bucket, no squeegee. There is only the empty air, and the can on the table, and the watch going twenty past six, and me, saying good evening to nothing, waiting for an answer that will not come, because the one who might have answered has gone to sleep in the corner of a room that no longer exists, in a town that has stopped being the case.

FINAL NOTE: THE SENTENCE THAT DOES NOT END

This novel was written in the language that knows about silence. It was written on a typewriter with an O key worn shiny, with a ribbon grey at the edges, with a platen that did not advance for a fortnight. It was written in the white space after the negation, in the silence that is not empty but full, in the waiting that is not passive but the most active thing a person can do. It was written by a man who was a draft, who became a man, who finished the sentence that had been waiting for eleven years, who woke up in an ordinary room with a watch that goes and a can on the table.

The novel does not end. The novel is the sentence *It does not end* made into a book. The novel is the white space after the negation, filled with words. The novel is the can on the table, sealed, labelled *Third of March, NEARLY*, waiting for a verb that never comes. The novel is the elephant in the corner, opening his eyes, looking at the reader with the patience of a creature who has waited longer than language has existed. The novel is the watch that goes, the radio that stops at eleven, the neighbour who turns it off, the desk with four legs, the floor that is cold, the weather that does what weather does.

The novel does not end. The novel is the sentence that does not end. The novel is *It does not end.*

Nevermind.

*It does not end.*

THE ADVENTURES OF OLIVER ONIONS

A Novel of Silvora Reefs

Based on Malice in Underland

---

PART ONE: THE CRASH

You are lying on your back in shallow water, and the sky above is doing what it apparently always does: bruising slowly from violet into a crimson it never quite commits to. Something came down out of that sky. You were in it. The evidence for this is that you are wet, that the harbour is full of a smell like hot metal apologising, and that you can still hear rotor blades somewhere above the fog.

Your monocle is intact. Your watch says 3:33. Your pockets have been gone through by the tide and are still full of gold.

You do not know your name. You do not know this town's name, though the word *Silvora* is sitting at the front of your head with no paperwork to support it. Somewhere to your left, an elephant the colour of a bruise is waiting for you to finish a sentence you do not remember starting.

The elephant is purple. He carries an unused saddle. His name is Furthermore, and he is older than language. He regards words as a fad. His catchphrase is "Nevermind," delivered after suspenseful buildups that let all the air out at once. You have loved him for it and been maddened by it in roughly equal measure for forty-four years.

You stand up. The water comes to your waist. The cold has stopped being an event and become a condition.

Behind you, half in the shallows and half up the shingle where it stopped, is the thing you came down in. It is large and it is broken and it is broken in a way that objects of that size are not supposed to break. The metal is hot where the water is not touching it, and it ticks as it gives up that heat, unevenly, like a clock being wound by somebody who keeps stopping to think.

There is no cockpit. There are no controls, no seat, no restraints, no instrument housing, no place for a hand to have gone. There is no space inside it in which a person could have been sitting.

You were unmistakably inside it.

On one intact panel, stencilled small and neat and slightly off-square: a serial number. Under the serial number, four words in a language that resolves into English while you are reading it and declines to afterwards, so that you know you have understood them and cannot say what they were.

Your monocle. You raise it to your eye.

Through the gold, the wreck is not wreckage. It is a *correction*. Something struck out — heavily, repeatedly, with enough force to tear the surface it was being struck out on — and the tearing is what came down over the harbour, and the sound of it was rotors because that is the nearest sound a man has for it.

You were in the tear. Not in the machine; there was never a machine. You came through the hole made by somebody pressing too hard on a thing they were trying to remove.

Your pockets. Four items on a flat stone at the top of the shingle. The water did not take a single one of them — which is worth sitting with, because the water took your hat.

A gold monocle, unbroken, on a gold chain, in a case worn shiny at the hinge by a great many openings.

A gold pocket watch, stopped at 3:33, with the second hand held mid-sweep rather than resting against the pin. A spring runs down and the hand comes to rest. This one has been *held*.

A cane with a gold onion for a handle — a joke somebody made once, a long time ago, and then had to go on living inside for the rest of their life. The wear on the shaft says they lived inside it cheerfully.

And a notebook, waterlogged, ninety pages of it gone to a single blue smear. Three pages are still legible, dry in the middle of the ruin. The handwriting matches the callus on your finger — same pressure, same slight backward lean. You have no memory of writing them.

Through the monocle, the smeared pages stay smeared — but the blue moves. It is not ink dispersing in water. It is ink being *withdrawn*, page by page, back toward a nib, in a slow careful retraction that has been going on for some time and is nearly finished. The three dry pages are not survivors. They are what has not been taken back yet.

The third page has a list on it. Eleven names, in your hand, and nine of them have been crossed out — not scribbled, *ruled* through, once, neatly, the way a man crosses out something he is sorry about.

The harbour wall. Green lampposts go up a street in a line. Each one throws considerably more shadow than its flame can account for, and the shadows do not agree with one another about where the light is coming from.

The tide has left prints in the sand. They are yours. They go from the water to the wall in a straight line, without deviation, without the shortening and hesitation of a man on a wet slope in the dark. Walking, at an even stride, like a man crossing a room he knows the shape of.

Through the monocle there is only one set of prints on this beach and they go the wrong way — down the sand, into the water, in a stride that does not vary — and the sea end of them is where the wreck is, and there is no return.

Reconstructed from stride and sand: your prints do not emerge from the sea. They begin four feet out, under three feet of water, already walking. A second set in a worn-heeled boot comes down to meet them and hurries back up the hill alongside.

You walk up the street, past the green lamps, into the parish.

PART TWO: THE PARISH

Silvora Reefs exists beneath a perpetual twilight sky painted in deep purples and crimsons. Fog drifts through the streets with unusual persistence, never quite clearing. Clocks throughout the town rarely agree on the time. Buildings demonstrate interior spaces that feel more expansive than their external dimensions suggest. Corridors sometimes lead to unexpected destinations.

The air itself carries an unusual weight, thick with unspoken history.

You find your flat at Hang-Hi Quiet Apartments. The second floor hangs from something overhead rather than standing on anything below. Your corridor runs from the stair to your door in a length that has never twice been the same.

Inside, the flat has arranged itself into the shape it uses when you have been away: chair angled toward the window, desk under its usual weight of paper, the smell of wet wool coming off the coat you have not yet taken off. The lamp is on. You do not recall leaving it on, but the lamp and you have an arrangement and it has never yet been in writing.

A tall spotted glass in the hallway. Hold still in front of it. On a long enough hold, the corridor behind your reflection stops being the corridor. It becomes a room with a low ceiling and one lamp with a green shade, and a desk under the lamp, and a great deal of paper on the desk, and a chair pushed back from it.

Through the monocle the gold lens declines to reflect the corridor at all. It gives you the room directly: a desk, a lamp with a green shade, a typewriter with a page in it, and the page has a paragraph on it, and the paragraph is about a man standing in a hallway looking into a mirror.

The snake on the building sign. HANG-HI QUIET APARTMENT — the gap at the end sized for a letter that is not there, because the final S was supplied years ago by somebody with a spray can, a steady hand and a considerable sense of humour. It is a snake. Coiled into the shape of the letter. It has been in a different coil every single time you have looked at it, over eleven years, and you have never once caught it moving.

Through the monocle, the snake is not paint and is not a snake. It is a correction — a letter that was typed wrong, struck through, and typed again, and the strike-through is what you have been reading as a body all these years. It is still wet. It has been still wet since before you moved in.

Landy's repair notices on the corridor board. WINDOW 2F — WEATHER NONCONFORMING — RESOLVED. DOOR 2C — OPENS TO 3A ON THURSDAYS — RESOLVED. Each form stamped with a four-lobed seal: FILED WITH THE COALITION FOR CULPABILITY AND CONSTRUCTIVE CHANGE. NO FURTHER ACTION IS REQUIRED OF THE RESIDENT. NO FURTHER ACTION IS AVAILABLE TO THE RESIDENT.

Under the monocle, the word RESOLVED is not a word. It is the same three characters typed over and over on top of themselves — R, E, S, then back, then again — until the ribbon gave out and the paper gave up and agreed to be black in that shape. Twenty-two forms. Nothing was resolved. Something was *insisted on*, at length, by a machine, by somebody who could not fix the corridor and could not stop filling in the box.

Your desk. A door on two trestles. The left trestle is split along the grain and has not been mended; it has been shimmed, with something folded. You get underneath. The shim is a page. One of Landy's forms, filled in beautifully, in a hand considerably better than Landy's. Under NATURE OF FAULT it says: *structural, longstanding, load-bearing.* Under REMEDY it says: *none available. Recommend company.*

Through the monocle, the shim is not holding the desk up. The desk is holding the shim in. Somebody has kept a piece of paper somewhere it would be safe and dry for eleven years and has used a broken trestle as the excuse.

The window over the harbour. Rotors. Slow ones — the heavy, unhurried, entirely confident chop of a machine holding a position rather than travelling anywhere. Four days, at a constant bearing and a constant volume. Nobody else has mentioned it.

Open the window. With the window open the rotors resolve into what they have been all along, which is not rotors. It is a page being turned. Over and over, at the pace of a man checking something he has already read, from a room that is not in the direction the sound is coming from.

The clean rectangle on the floor by the window. Four small round depressions at its corners, pressed down into the grain over a period long enough that the wood has stopped intending to come back up. Something heavy stood here for years. Through the monocle the rectangle is not empty. There is a machine in it, and it is being used, and the man using it has his back to you and does not turn round.

Reconstruct the removal. It went straight up. A dragged object leaves a lip of dust at the trailing edge and there is neither, on any of four sides. Somebody lifted a machine that takes two men, vertically, without tilting it. And the dust inside the rectangle is *younger* than the dust outside. It has been removed and returned many times. The most recent removal was inside the last fortnight and it has not come back.

The other tenants. Nine floors hanging off nothing. A radio on four, a bath running on six, somebody moving furniture on two. In eleven years you have never met or named a single one.

Through the monocle there is nobody on any of the floors. The radio on four is playing to an empty room. The bath on six is running into a bath nobody is going to get into. It has been doing this for as long as the building has needed to sound occupied. The gold shows you the schedule. It was written by somebody who has lived in a full building and remembered what one sounds like.

Reaching for Hang-Hi the way you would reach for the parish returns nothing — while the rest of the town arrives at full strength from streets away. The radio and the bath have nobody behind them. Asimov's office is the last occupied room.

Asimov Sobeleaux. Manager. Former fisherman and cartographer. His explanations make perfect sense while being heard but become impossible to recall afterward. His office holds charts of waterways that appear on no map.

"Ah. The tenant who survives things. Come in, come in — mind the corridor, it is longer today than it was contracted to be."

He explains the impossible so clearly that the explanation will not survive the walk to your door. "One man needs more corridor than four men do. One man has further to go."

Luigi Luigi. Janitor. Patron Saint of the Squeegee. Cleans anomalies away without knowing they are anomalies. Rooms he has just done behave themselves.

"Ey! Mister Onions! You walk on the wet floor, you take the consequences up with a higher authority. Me, I only do the floor."

Lying Landy. The Unhandy Dandy. Every repair creates a new inconsistency. Immaculately dressed for a man under a sink.

"Mr Onions! Two-F! Now — the window. I've been at the window. It's sorted. It's completely sorted and I'd not want you to worry about the weather in it."

You walk the parish. Durry Kwain. Darek Dwayne Wayne. His heavy Southern drawl makes his name sound exactly like the sign above his head. He answers questions a beat before they are asked.

"Well, hey there, Ols. Sorry about your wait. — Ah. You ain't waited yet. Gimme a second, I'm runnin' a touch ahead this evenin'."

You ask about the harbour. "Nothin' come *down* over that harbour. Not last week, not last year, not never in my life. You came *up*."

You ask what you said coming up the hill. He stops wiping. "Wasn't the alphabet. You said your own name. Over and over, all the way up the hill in the truck, backwards. *Snoino Revilo. Snoino Revilo.* Like it was somebody you were tryin' to warn me about."

Snoino Revilo. Oliver Onions. Letter for letter, both ways.

The rotors. "Everybody hears it. Every soul on this hill hears it, all day, every day. And we don't say it, Ols, 'cause the first fella who says it out loud is gonna make it *true*. Right now it's a noise. You say 'there's a helicopter over the harbour' in this town, in a voice, where folks can hear you — and then there's a helicopter over the harbour, and it's got to have come from someplace, and it's got to be *for* somebody. So we let it be a noise. That's the whole town's plan."

You carry it instead. You take the weight off the counter and put it somewhere in yourself where it will keep. Darek notices, a beat late, which is not how Darek notices anything. "You just did somethin', Ols. I don't know what it was. I'd like the record to show I noticed it."

The hallway. Walk it with Darek. Eleven feet. Walk it alone. Thirty-odd. The variable is not the hallway.

"Whatever it is you're workin' at — you got a standin' invitation to be in here doin' it. Sit at the window. Talk at me. Just don't do the whole thing alone, huh. On account of, apparently, that's how the hallway gets long."

The Möbius Strip Club. Ginny 'Gin' Ginja. Red hair pinned like a threat. She manages the dimensional anomaly with fierce precision.

"You're standing in my doorway with your weight on the back foot and your eyes doing the room — left, up, left again, exits. That's a guard. That's a man expecting to be hit by architecture."

The corridor doesn't turn and brings you back facing forward. The wall behind the far booth goes transparent, roughly nine times a night, roughly two seconds a go. Once a month it shows something else: a room. Low ceiling. One green lamp. A desk. A chair pulled out. Nobody sitting in it.

Describe the room. "Desk made out of a door on two trestles. Left trestle's split. Paper everywhere — not stacked, *drifted*. Green shade on the lamp. Cup with a ring under it. Chair pushed back. And a typewriter. That's your desk, isn't it. I've been looking at your desk through my back wall once a month for eleven years."

The sign. Through the monocle: REVILO'S TRAP BUILT.

Ginny doesn't own the club. Instructions arrive on four-lobed cards. For eleven years they have described a tall man in a coat with an onion-handled cane, and what to do if he walks in the front. Let him in. Let him look. Do not stop him. Do not help him.

This week the Insta-gators received a card she has not been shown.

The ice machine. The drain runs uphill for eleven inches. "That pipe was straight in March. I had it in. I watched him fit it. It ran downhill because pipes do. So my building's been quietly redecorating the plumbing and the only reason anybody knows is that ice is the only thing in here that won't lie about which way is down."

The wall's transparency ran at four seconds until the day you came out of the harbour. It has been two every night since.

"Guard up, Onions. Whatever you're walking toward — it's going to end in a room with one other person in it. They always do. And in a room with one other person, authority isn't the loud thing. It's whoever's still there at the end of the sentence. Be there at the end of the sentence."

Rest Assured. Soliloquy. Hannah O'Dochtaigh. Keeper of the vaults. Speaks in riddles, puns, and speeches addressed to nobody.

"You have come to the house of the horizontal. Mind the threshold. It is the only part of my establishment that has ever asked anything of anybody, and what it asks is very small: that you notice which way you are going through it."

The plates carry your name. Including a middle name nobody here knows. "Everyone sees their own name. It is a mirror, Mr Onions. A brass one. Mirrors are not clever. They are only very, very attentive."

"A mirror doesn't know a middle name I haven't said aloud in thirty years." The rhyme stops. "No. It does not."

The plates do not reflect the reader. They display whatever name is currently being written. It is yours only while you are looking.

Forty interments paid for in advance by the Coalition. They talked. They described the town's impossibilities out loud, repeatedly. Then a four-lobed card arrived, and then they did.

You have been saying it out loud. To Asimov. To Darek. To her.

There is a vault at the end of the west corridor with no plate on it. "There is no vault at the end of the west corridor. There are eleven vaults."

Mrs Ahearne's door has no plate. It has never had provision for one. "No. It has not. I keep it shut. I have kept it shut since before you moved into Sobeleaux's hanging floor. It stands ajar because it was left ajar, by somebody who meant to come straight back, and has not, and the width has not changed in eleven years. I have measured the width every Michaelmas. It is the only measurement in this town that has never once disagreed with itself."

When you look, look at the *page*. Not the desk. Not the lamp. The page. And whatever is on it — do not read it aloud.

"Because forty people in my ledger said it out loud. Saying a thing aloud in Silvora Reefs is how a thing gets to be the case. It is the local physics. Bud Onkuhdonk holds up the Outside Inn with his mouth. The Coalition are clerks who arrive afterwards."

"Then I will keep it. All of it. Until I know what I want to be true."

"Then you are the first. Forty of them, sir, and every single one told somebody. Not out of foolishness — out of *relief*. It is an unbearable amount to carry alone and the carrying is the whole of the price. Carry it a while longer. It is worth what it costs."

Cheeses Thrice Pizza. Woolbinkle. Bull antelope. Thick wool-like fur. Antlers that go the wrong way. Obsessed with canning, containment, preservation.

"Mr Onions! I had a feeling. I had a feeling and I put one on and if I was wrong I'd have eaten it myself, so there's no — there's no obligation, is what I'm saying."

The cheese patterns reflect customers' deeply held fears and desires. When you order, melted cheese briefly forms patterns resembling manuscript pages. "Your thoughts are particularly spicy today."

The shelf behind him isn't food. Forty cans. "I can things. Something happens and it's got a shape and if you leave it out it goes off, or worse, it goes *vague*, and vague is worse than off because you can throw off away. So I get it in a can while it's still got its shape."

March. Patience. Four hours of a Tuesday in the one at the end he would not sell.

"Can't Elope Antelope." The poem about the worst thing he ever did. "She never said nope. She said *not yet*. And whoever wrote it changed it to nope for the rhyme, and it is a better rhyme, and I have spent eleven years understanding that it is a better rhyme."

Everything here goes vague. The menus, the hallways, the count of the vaults. But his cans hold. Eleven years, forty cans, not one gone vague.

"If *I* can make something in this town that holds — then holding is possible here, Mr Onions, and if holding is possible then somebody has been choosing not to."

"You are not nobody." The lights come up about a quarter. The far wall comes in three feet. The room gets warmer. "Nobody's ever said that to me about anything. I've had a lot of things given back to me over the years. Given back is a whole category and I know it well. I've never had one *left* with me."

Giddyup End 'em Ribs. Ben. Wutchu 'Ben' Uptu. Eighty-eight. Sits at the middle of a twenty-two foot table with both palms flat.

"What you been up to that you ain't told nobody."

The masks on the walls. Portraits of people you know. They arrive on the step in the morning, unwrapped, unlabelled, always depicting a living Reefer. One came thirty years ago and it came *wrong*. No spot for it on the wall. Ben held it in the doorway for two hours and put it behind the fisherman. Has been not-deciding for thirty years.

Names carved under the table. Nineteen sets of initials. A gap for a twentieth. The wood there is clean. Kept clean.

Your name is under there. The date beside it moves. Only goes one way. When a number gets small the person starts coming to Giddyup more.

"What is it like?" "It ain't knowing. It's *remembering*. I remember your mother at a table with her back to a door. I remember it the way I remember my own wedding. And I wasn't there. About a third of 'em ain't mine, and I have never been able to tell you which third."

"A woman at a table with her back to a door, saying 'And furthermore—'." His palms come off the table. In fifty years they have not come off for anything short of a funeral. "In *your* voice. She said it in *your* voice. It's yours."

The Outside Inn. Barney. Josh 'Barney' Arneaux. Indoor weather. Trees, streams, a sky that changes independently.

Bud Onkuhdonk. Bellhop. Begins every response with BUT. Denies anomalies aggressively. "BUT — before you say anything — everything in this establishment is completely normal and I'd thank you to note that I said it first."

Dee Stroii. A guest, or two. A narrow man in a good coat, standing by the stream. Also sitting by the stair. Two of them. Not twins. When one raises a hand the other raises the other.

Chef D.'s Lishush Mush. Chef Delilah 'Dee' Rishrush. Toothless. Speech renders words as a mouth without teeth renders them. Foods that trigger cross-sensory experiences.

T. Sous chef. Alliterative culinary soliloquies. Utensils occasionally leave the counter.

Dude's Dews & Don'ts. Mary Guana. Iguana. Bloodshot. Complex arbitrary ordering procedures. Neon sign alternates spellings.

The G.'s Spot. Wino-T. Antonio 'Wino-T' Vino. Former rapper. "Why not?" Mirrors that don't show accurate reflections. Sometimes display events occurring elsewhere. Sometimes show Oliver typing at a desk rather than sitting in the barber's chair.

Sir Jerry. Barber-surgeon. Medieval knight-speak crossed with modern clinical terminology. "The presenting field."

Monsieur Albert. Owlbear. In the third mirror. Impeccably courteous. Has been in the shop longer than the shop.

"How long have you been in the mirror?" "Longer than the shop. I recall being *in the room* for that, monsieur, and standing on the boards, and being handed a cup."

"You were in the room." "I was mentioned less. Then only in passing. Then as a feature rather than a patron. At each stage I was a little further into the glass. One cannot lodge a complaint about a diminution in mentions. There is no office."

"You are being mentioned a very great deal at present. In this shop, at the Inn, at the mausoleum, in the pizzeria. A man who is mentioned a great deal is a man who is *in* the room. But mentions may be withdrawn. That is the whole of my counsel: they may be withdrawn, monsieur, and there is no office, and it does not hurt."

"Why only in the glass?" "Because the glass will have me. A mirror does not select. The room has *taste*."

The mirror's opinion: *he is good company and he is the only thing in here that has ever said thank you to a surface, and he has been getting quieter for years, and I cannot make him louder, I can only refuse to stop including him, and I have never once stopped.*

"What have you seen?" "From *in here* he is not at a desk in another room. He is in this one. At the desk in the middle of this shop, between the chairs, where there is no desk — and Antonio walks through the place where the desk is forty times a day and has never once broken stride."

Ask him to turn. Albert addresses the empty air between the chairs: "Sir. There is a gentleman here who would like a word."

In the third mirror, the man at the desk stops. For four seconds a room that has been typing for a fortnight does not type. Then it starts again, faster, and does not stop.

"He heard. Forty years of being a feature, and it transpires that the whole apparatus can be interrupted by an owlbear being *polite* at the correct piece of empty air. I shall be doing that again. Every evening at six. It is not much of a campaign. It is, however, entirely mine."

You have walked through that air. Twice. The temperature was wrong on the far side. Not cold. *Later.* You came out into a room that had been going on for a few seconds longer.

PART THREE: THE INVESTIGATION

The beats accumulate. Clues do not gate; they accumulate.

**The Crash** — Something came down over Silvora Reefs. You were in it. But nothing came down. You came up. The bruising is symmetrical — held, then let go, not a fall. The prints begin at depth, mid-stride. A second set in a worn-heeled boot meets you and hurries back up the hill alongside.

**The Sound of Typing** — Typewriter keys where there is no typewriter. An office behind the mirrors. The rotor sound is a page being turned. The ice machine drain runs uphill. The snake on the sign is a wet correction. The hallway is eleven feet with a friend, thirty-odd alone. The wall transparency lasts two seconds — tuned to a glance. The week before you surfaced it was four. The mirror shows a room nine feet by eleven. The desk is a door with the ghost of 2-F.

**The Coalition** — Someone is filing paperwork about the impossible. The four-lobed stamp on every repair form. Raised and closed the same day, no work recorded. Two a year for eleven years. Ginny's cards. Soliloquy's prepaid interments — forty people who talked, then cards arrived, then they died. The licence at Durry Kwain: *Dimensional variance within permitted tolerance. Proprietor not to be informed.*

**O.R.A.C.L.E.** — A countdown to an activation, for a machine nobody can point to. The Operant Reality Alteration and Causality Linking Engine is not a physical device. It is a manifestation of your subconscious attempt to make sense of the surreal. The Quad-C is your analytical mind trying to impose order. The countdown is counting down to the moment of possible awakening.

**The Man in the Warped Mirror** — Green with purple accents. Your cadence. Your vocabulary. Snoino Revilo. Oliver Onions backwards. He stands at a window that is not a window. He has kept a chair for a visitor who is himself for eleven years. The waste basket holds two hundred drafts of your arrival, each naming you more kindly: the tenant, then Onions, then Oliver, then Ols. The last one, four days ago, calls you Ols — what Darek calls you.

**The Last Word** — The page in the machine says *and he does not*. It stops mid-sentence. The sentence describing your arrival is unfinished because you arrived, and the writing of you and the being of you cannot happen in the same second. One of you has to finish it. Whoever finishes it keeps the language. The other becomes something that has been described.

PART FOUR: THE OFFICE

You stand in the hallway. The mirror has stopped bothering. It shows the *edge between them* — about four inches wide, with no thickness at all. The office is on the other side. The lamp is on. The chair is pushed back. A page in the machine with type on it. Nothing is stopping you.

You step through.

No membrane. No cold. No drama. Your hallway is behind you — the coat rack, the green flame, eleven years of coming home — and then it is not. The last thing is the sound of a bucket being set down two floors below and a squeegee starting an arc.

You are standing on boards in a room nine feet by eleven. A green-shaded lamp on a desk that is a door. A chair pushed back. A man at a wall with his back to you who does not turn round and does not appear surprised.

The floor on this side is the same floor. Same width of board, same grain. It runs under the frame without a join.

He turns. It is your face. Not a resemblance. Your face, with everything moved a sixteenth of an inch toward the mirror image and stopped short.

"Well. Now we have both had a very long day."

You sit in the second chair. "Eleven years I have kept a chair in a room nine feet by eleven, in which there is no reason whatsoever for there to be two chairs, and every visitor I have ever had has been me, coming in, and sitting in the other one."

"Say your name."

"You know it. You have known it since a man drove you up a hill in a truck at four in the morning while you said it over and over, backwards, out of the water, trying to warn him about somebody. Say it, if it helps."

"Snoino Revilo. Oliver Onions. Letter for letter, both ways."

"Letter for letter. Not an anagram — an anagram is a coincidence with good manners. A reversal is an author. Silvo and Ovlis founded that town twice, eleven years apart, on the same plots, and neither charter mentions the other. That was not cleverness. That was a man who could not settle on a name and did not have the discipline to throw the first one away, and the parish has been living in both of them ever since. I have never thrown anything away. You will have noticed the basket. That is not the same as keeping things, and it has taken me eleven years to learn the difference, and I learned it about four days ago and I am not enjoying it."

"You've been writing me."

"I have been writing you. Yes. *You are not a man.* You are a draft. You are a well-made one, you are the best thing I have ever done and I have done nothing else, and you are two hundred pages and a set of habits and a stopped watch, and when you tell me you are frightened what is happening is that I have written down that you are frightened. There is no fact underneath you. I have looked. I built the looking."

"Yes. And so are you."

He stops. Four full seconds. "Go on."

"You stand at a window that is a wall. You keep a chair for a visitor who is yourself. You have a cup with a ring under it older than the cup and you have never once left this room to get another one, because there is no out there for you to get one in. You are nine feet by eleven and a set of habits, and when I came through that seam your sentence stopped in the middle, because the writing of a man and the being of him cannot happen in the same second — and it stopped on *your* side. One of us is standing in a room he can leave."

He looks at the page. "That is the argument I have been unable to write down for eleven years, and you have made it in a room with no window, at eleven o'clock at night, on a knee that has been going since March. I would like it on record that I am not angry. I have simply been beaten with my own vocabulary and there is nobody to complain to."

"They have insides. I have been in them."

Monsieur Albert was not put in a mirror. He was mentioned less, over years, and at each stage there was nothing to object to and no office to object at, and he has been courteous for forty years and asks nothing except to be addressed.

Dee Stroii was described, and the description stopped, and started again better, and the first one was left in — not by accident. By somebody who could not take it out. He has been the leftover draft of a man for nineteen years and he would rather that than the alternative.

Bud Onkuhdonk says 'it's a light well' out loud every morning because the one year he did not, a floor went, with four guests on it. He is holding up a building with his mouth.

Woolbinkle has canned forty things in eleven years and every one has held, in a town where nothing holds, because he did one unforgivable thing once and has been making sure nothing else got loose ever since.

"You did not put insides in them. They grew them. In the gaps. While you were busy."

He has both hands flat on the desk. "I have kept you alive. The flood took ninety pages and went round three. The vault at the mausoleum has stood two fingers open for eleven years because a woman I made has been keeping it shut for you and I let her. There are twenty-two maintenance forms recommending that you not walk that corridor alone, and I wrote the first one, and a man in a very good suit has been copying it out for eleven years without knowing what he was copying. I could have ended it at any point. It would have taken one sentence. I have written the sentence two hundred times and it is in the basket by your foot."

"I know. You have been very bad at hiding it. The shim in the trestle is a form you kept somewhere dry for eleven years, and the desk is not holding the shim up, the desk is holding the shim *in*, and you have refused to have the leg mended by a man who has offered every year. The drafts are dated eleven years apart at the bottom and four days apart at the top. That is not a writer's block. That is somebody finding one more thing to do. And the earliest ones call me the tenant. And the last one calls me Ols, which is what a man calls me who drove down to a slipway at four in the morning."

"I did not think anybody was going to read the basket. That is the whole of my defence and it is not a good one."

A long silence. The longest he has gone without writing in eleven years. The town did not stop. "I have been under the impression that it required me continuously. It appears that it requires me *approximately*, and I would have liked to know that at some point during the first decade."

The last word. "The page in the machine says *and he does not*, and it has said that since the moment you came through. One of us has to finish the sentence. It cannot be finished by both and it cannot be left, because a thing left in the present continuous does not stay still, it *frays*. Whoever finishes it keeps the language. The other one becomes something that has been described. I am not going to advise you. Take the chair or don't."

Furthermore is in the room. He has been in the room since you came through the seam, standing where the wall is, with an unused saddle and the whole of his enormous patience, and he is drawing himself up.

PART FIVE: THE ENDINGS

**ENDING ONE: WAKE**

You sit down and finish the sentence yourself. Wake up.

"Yes. That is the correct one. Silvora Reefs stops. Not violently — it will simply not be the case, the way a thing is not the case when nobody is putting it. The green lamps do not go out, they were never lit, and the harbour was never wet, and the fog that never quite cleared did not clear because it was a clause in a sentence somebody has now stopped writing. Darek Dwayne Wayne, who drove down to a slipway at four in the morning, has the cone made before the bell over the door has finished. And then he does not, and then there is no bell, and then there is no door."

You wake up. An ordinary room. Not nine feet by eleven. A window with weather behind it doing what weather does, unbeautifully, without a front coming through the lounge at ten and without anybody's voice changing on the word sky. Your watch is on the table. Twenty past six. The second hand is going, sweeping, arriving somewhere and leaving it.

You went and got the language back, and it cost the town, and the town was made of you, and so was the cost. Nobody is going to be able to tell you whether that was the right thing. The only two people who could have argued it out are now one person, in an ordinary room, with a watch on the table that is going.

You will put the argument to yourself at intervals for the rest of your life. You will take both sides. You are extremely good at taking both sides; you have had eleven years of practice at it and you did not know that was what you were practising.

**ENDING TWO: STAY**

You push the chair back toward him. Let him finish it.

He sits down. It takes him a moment, and the moment is not hesitation; it is a man sitting down in a chair he has not sat in for eleven years and finding the height is wrong for him now. He puts his hands on the machine. They are your hands with everything a sixteenth of an inch the other way.

And he finishes the sentence. You do not hear what he writes. You are not going to hear what he writes, ever, on any evening, in any town. Because the thing about being described is that it is the least noticeable event available to a person. Nothing happens. Nothing has ever happened. It has been happening to you all your life at a rate of several thousand words a day and you have never once felt it land.

You come out of the seam at Durry Kwain. "Sorry about your wait." And it lands. For the first time in eleven years the timing on that line is exactly, unremarkably right.

The rotors stop over the harbour that night and nobody mentions it. The count in the west corridor settles at nineteen and stays there. The neon at Mary's says DUDE'S DEWS & DON'TS and goes on saying it. The hallway at Durry Kwain is eleven feet whether you walk it alone or not.

It is a good town. It has been made very carefully by somebody who has had eleven years of practice and two hundred drafts and one enormous piece of information about what it costs, and he is not going to waste it.

You gave the last word away to the only other person alive who could have used it, and you got a town back, and everybody in it is going to be all right. The decision will never be one you can point to afterwards. Somebody is being very careful with you. He is going to go on being very careful with you for a long time, and he is good at it, and you are never going to notice — and that is not a failure of yours. It is the whole of his craft, and he learned it on you.

**ENDING THREE: TOGETHER**

"Two chairs. You have kept two chairs in this room for eleven years, in which there was never going to be a visitor, and in which there was never going to be *room* for a visitor — and you have never once asked yourself why, because asking yourself why is my half of the job."

He sits down in his own chair. After a while you sit down in the other one. Nobody has ever sat in it. The cushion has not gone down anywhere.

The sentence gets finished by two people. It takes a considerable amount of time. He argues for a semicolon and you argue for letting the line breathe, and you argue about it for the better part of an hour, and at some point the page acquires a second line, and neither of you can afterwards say who put it there.

Silvora Reefs does not stop. It *settles*. The hallway at Durry Kwain finds a length and keeps it. The count in the west corridor comes out at nineteen twice running and Soliloquy stands very still and does not say anything in couplets about it. A snake on a sign holds its coil. The ink on it dries. It has been wet for eleven years and it dries in about four minutes.

Woolbinkle was right. If holding is possible here, then somebody had been choosing not to. The somebody was two people. Neither of them could do it alone. Both of them had spent eleven years being perfectly certain that the failure was a failure of will.

Neither of you got the last word. That was the arrangement. It is the only outcome in which the man with the ice cream is still standing behind his counter, running a touch previous, apologising for a wait that has never happened to anybody. The remedy was never available. That is the thing the form got right, in a hand better than Landy's, eleven years ago: *none available. Recommend company.*

**ENDING FOUR: NEVERMIND**

You say nothing. Let the elephant finish it.

Furthermore draws himself up. The air thickens. Something vast is being assembled behind his soft eyes, something that has been waiting since before there were words to wait in—

"Finally," he says, and goes to sleep in the corner.

"—say. And he says it. Not the word. He has been not-saying the word for as long as you have had a mind for him to not-say it in. And every single time the build has been enormous. The air thickens, something vast assembles itself behind those soft eyes, the whole room leans in — and then one flat syllable, delivered without emphasis, letting all the air out at once. *Nevermind.*"

It is not in words. He is older than language. It arrives as weight, and warmth at the back of the neck, and the particular pressure behind the sternum that you have been calling him since you were eight years old: *You have both been in here talking to yourselves. I have been in here the whole time. Somebody has been listening. That was never the part that was in doubt.*

Revilo sits down on the floor. Not the chair. "I have been so *lonely*, and there has been an elephant."

The page in the machine is not finished by either of you. It is taken out, carefully, by four hands, and folded twice, and put in a plastic wallet in an inside pocket over the heart where it lives — and the sentence stays in the present continuous, unresolved, fraying gently, exactly the way this town has always run.

Silvora Reefs was never being held together by an author. It was being held together by somebody being listened to. It has been over-engineered for a decade and it turns out the load-bearing element was an irrelevant elephant with an unused saddle who never once left.

The sentence never got finished and nobody kept the language. The whole apparatus — the countdown cut into brass by hand, the Coalition and its four-lobed cards, the forty vaults settled in advance, the eleven years of somebody being extremely careful about a man who did not know he was being kept — turned out to be two halves of one person not talking to each other.

In front of a witness. Who had been standing in the room the entire time, older than language and unimpressed by it, waiting for one of them to stop making a case long enough for somebody else to say something. He waited since before either of them could speak. He was never once in a hurry about it. That was the point, and it took forty-four years and a purple elephant to make it, and it was made in the end without a single word.

EPILOGUE: THE NOTEBOOK

Three pages, dry. A watch stopped mid-sweep at twenty-seven minutes past three. A monocle in a case worn shiny at the hinge. And nothing whatsoever with a name on it.

The third page has a list. Eleven names. Nine ruled through. The two that remain: Oliver Onions. Snoino Revilo.

Or perhaps: Oliver Onions. Furthermore.

Or perhaps: The Writer. The Written.

Or perhaps: The Man Who Walks. The Man Who Waits.

The gold monocle shows the ink under your nails is not ink. It is the same substance the sky over this harbour is bruising with — the identical violet-going-to-crimson, at the identical depth — and it is not on the nails, it is *under* them, in the bed, in the quick. And there is a great deal more of it in you than there is under the nails. It goes up the finger. It is in the hand to the wrist and past it, and the gold declines to show you where it stops.

The typewriter ticks once as it cools, which it should not do, because nothing in this room has been warm.

The snake on the sign recoils. Same paint, same flaking at the tail, same wall — and a different S, in a slightly different place, doing a slightly different amount of work to hold the name together.

The corridor gives you a different length again — shorter this time, companionably so, which from this building reads less like an anomaly and more like a courtesy extended to a tenant who has had a long day.

Somewhere below, a squeegee completes an arc, and for a moment the whole stairwell agrees to be a stairwell — the angles settle, the flight lands where the eye expects, the handrail runs true from top to bottom. It holds for about as long as the wet takes to dry.

The lamp does not flicker. Nothing in this room does anything at all unless it is described, and neither of you is describing anything at the moment, and so the room simply holds.

Out beyond the walls, at an enormous distance and through no window, a town continues — a queue moving, a squeegee arcing, a pan saying shhhk — and you can hear all of it at once and none of it individually.

He shifts his weight from one foot to the other at the wall that is not a window, and the boards under him take it without a sound, because they have taken it for eleven years and know the shape of him.

The page in the machine says *and he does not*.

And he does not.

And he does.

And they do.

And furthermore—

Nevermind.

*Finis.*
